Algorithm Theory & Dithering Guide

Dithering is a mathematical technique used to create the illusion of continuous tone images using a limited color palette—in this case, 1-bit monochrome (pure black and white).

Error Diffusion Algorithms

Quantizing continuous grayscale pixels ($0\dots255$) down to pure 1-bit binary introduces severe quantization error. Error diffusion algorithms calculate this remainder ($e = \text{old\_pixel} - \text{new\_pixel}$) and distribute fractions of $e$ to neighboring unvisited pixels ahead of the scanline.

  • Floyd-Steinberg & Jarvis-Judice-Ninke (JJN): These classic kernels distribute the quantization error across adjacent and subsequent scanlines, producing smooth organic textures and preserving fine detail at the cost of sequential processing overhead.
  • Atkinson Dithering: Originally developed by Bill Atkinson for early Macintosh systems, this algorithm distributes only $3/4$ of the error to surrounding pixels. The remaining $1/4$ is discarded, causing highlights and deep shadows to blow out with crisp, high-contrast aesthetics.

Ordered Dithering (Bayer Matrix)

Unlike error diffusion which processes pixels sequentially, ordered dithering compares each pixel against a fixed threshold matrix ($M_{n\times n}$). Because every pixel is calculated independently of its neighbors, Bayer dithering ($2\times2$, $4\times4$, and $8\times8$) is computationally extremely fast and produces stable, non-flickering crosshatch patterns—making it ideal for real-time video feeds and camera processing.

Lo-Fi Audio PCM Downsampling

Reducing audio bit depth to 8-bit, 4-bit, or 2-bit introduces characteristic quantization noise and bit-crushed harmonic distortion typical of retro game consoles, early arcade hardware, and vintage digital samplers.
